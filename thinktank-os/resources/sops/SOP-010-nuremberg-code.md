# SOP-010 · The Nuremberg Code

**Status:** DRAFT v0.1 · 2026-10-10 · Not binding until approved
**Owner:** Department Director (Research) · **Approver:** President

## Purpose

Every protection in SOP-006 descends from this document. The Nuremberg Code is the origin point of modern research ethics — written in the verdict of a war-crimes trial, not in a committee room. Everyone doing human-subjects work at Glenride knows what it says and why it exists. History is the reason the rules are non-negotiable.

## Scope

Required knowledge for anyone on human-subjects work (see SOP-006 and SOP-008). Referenced wherever consent, risk, or participant rights are at issue.

## Procedure

1. **Know the history.** During World War II, physicians of the Nazi regime conducted lethal experiments on concentration-camp prisoners — freezing, high-altitude, sterilization, infectious disease, and others — without consent and without scientific justification. After the war, the United States tried 23 defendants (20 of them physicians) in the Doctors' Trial at Nuremberg (December 1946 – August 1947). Seven were sentenced to death. In delivering the verdict, the judges set out ten principles for permissible human experimentation. That text is the Nuremberg Code (1947).

2. **Know the ten points.** Each is stated here in operational form:
   1. **Voluntary consent is absolutely essential.** The subject must have legal capacity, be free from force, fraud, deceit, duress, or coercion, and understand enough to make an enlightened decision.
   2. **Fruitful results for the good of society.** The experiment must aim at knowledge unprocurable by other means — not curiosity, not convenience.
   3. **Built on prior knowledge.** Animal experimentation and understanding of the disease's natural history come first. Humans are never the pilot study.
   4. **Avoid all unnecessary suffering.** Physical and mental suffering and injury are designed out, not accepted as overhead.
   5. **No experiment expected to kill or disable.** If there is prior reason to believe death or disabling injury will result, it does not proceed — except where the investigators themselves serve as subjects.
   6. **Risk proportionate to importance.** The degree of risk taken must never exceed the humanitarian importance of the problem the experiment addresses.
   7. **Protection against remote harm.** Proper preparations and adequate facilities guard against even unlikely injury, disability, or death.
   8. **Qualified investigators only.** The work is conducted by scientifically qualified persons exercising the highest degree of skill and care.
   9. **The subject can stop at any time.** During the experiment, the subject must be at liberty to end it if continuation seems impossible.
   10. **The scientist must stop when harm is likely.** If, in the investigator's good-faith judgment, continuation is likely to cause injury, disability, or death, the experiment ends.

3. **Know the lineage.** The Code was the first stone; the structure built on it:
   - **Declaration of Helsinki (1964)** — the World Medical Association's ethical principles for medical research involving humans, revised repeatedly since.
   - **The Belmont Report (1979)** — the US response to Tuskegee and other abuses: respect for persons, beneficence, justice.
   - **The Common Rule (45 CFR 46)** — the federal regulatory implementation, revised 2018.
   - **ICH E6 Good Clinical Practice** — the international operational standard, now at R3 (2025).
   Each generation restates the Code's core in the language of its era. The core has not changed: **the person comes before the protocol.**

4. **Apply it.** When a consent question, risk question, or "can we do this" question arises and the regulations feel abstract, return to the ten points. They are plain language on purpose. If the proposed work fails point 1, 5, 6, 9, or 10, it stops — no committee vote required to know that.
