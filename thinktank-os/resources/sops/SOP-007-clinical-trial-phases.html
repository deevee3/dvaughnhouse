SOP-007 · Clinical Trial Phases & Fundamentals

Status: DRAFT v0.1 · 2026-10-10 · Not binding until approved Owner: Department Director (Research) · Approver: President

Purpose

One shared language for what a trial is and where it sits in development. Glenride evaluates clinical-stage assets, trial data, and development programs — everyone doing that work uses the same phase definitions and design vocabulary, so a "Phase II readout" means the same thing in every room.

Scope

Any Glenride evaluation of clinical trials, development-stage assets, or trial-derived data, whether Glenride sponsors the work or analyzes someone else's.

Procedure

  1. Know the phases. Each phase answers a different question. Do not treat them as interchangeable.
  2. Phase 0 (exploratory IND) — microdosing in a handful of subjects. Question: does the drug behave in humans the way the models predicted? No therapeutic intent.
  3. Phase I — first-in-human, typically 20–100 healthy volunteers (patients in oncology). Question: is it safe, and what does the body do to the drug (pharmacokinetics)? Dose escalation.
  4. Phase II — ~100–300 patients with the target condition. Question: does it work, and at what dose? Efficacy signal plus side-effect profile. Often split into IIa (dosing) and IIb (efficacy).
  5. Phase III — hundreds to thousands of patients, often multi-center and multi-country. Question: does it work better than the standard of care, at scale? These are the pivotal trials that support regulatory approval.

  6. Phase IV (post-marketing) — after approval. Question: what happens in the real world, over longer time, in broader populations? Safety surveillance and label expansion.

  7. Know the regulatory gates. An IND (Investigational New Drug application) must be in effect before human testing begins in the US. Approval comes through an NDA (New Drug Application) for small molecules or a BLA (Biologics License Application) for biologics. Glenride does not file these; Glenride evaluates the evidence behind them.

  8. Know the design vocabulary. When reviewing or describing a trial, state each of these explicitly:

  9. Randomization — how participants were assigned to arms, or that they weren't.
  10. Blinding — open-label, single-blind, or double-blind; who knew what.
  11. Control — placebo, active comparator, or historical/external control.

  12. Endpoints — the pre-specified primary endpoint(s) first; secondary and exploratory after. An endpoint chosen after the data were seen is a hypothesis, not a result.

  13. Protocol is law. Deviations from the approved protocol are documented, explained, and assessed for impact on interpretability. A trial that didn't follow its protocol is a different trial than the one on paper — say so.

  14. GCP applies. Any interventional human trial work follows ICH E6(R3) Good Clinical Practice (adopted January 2025; FDA final guidance September 2025). Participant protection and data reliability are the twin obligations — see SOP-005 and SOP-006.

Records

  • For each trial evaluated: phase, design elements (step 3), regulatory status, and the evidence assessment — in the study record.

Review

Annually, or when ICH or FDA guidance changes the phase or GCP landscape.

Basis

FDA investigational-drug regulations (21 CFR Part 312); ICH E6(R3) Good Clinical Practice; ICH E8(R1) General Considerations for Clinical Studies.
