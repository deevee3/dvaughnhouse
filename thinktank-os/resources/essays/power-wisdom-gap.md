# The Power-Wisdom Gap and the Imperative for Independent Infrastructure
*The flagship essay. Expanded edition v2, October 2026. D'Vaughn House.*

## How this essay is built

This is a founder's thesis, not a journal article — but it is argued the way I want Glenride's scholars to argue: every empirical claim wired to a real source, every source verified to exist, every limit stated plainly. Sources are labeled by type: peer-reviewed work, preprints (not yet peer-reviewed), institutional reports, and recognized classics. Where a finding is correlational, contested, or an estimate rather than a measurement, I say so. Several terms here are my own framework language — *power-wisdom gap*, *cognitive habitat degradation*, *Agentic Enterprise*, *Multi-Agent Collaborative Thought Graph (MaCTG)*, *Cold Start Context Loss*, *flourishing stewardship* — proposed as analytical constructs, not established terms. Two words you will not find: "sovereign" and "humanism." The first carries baggage I won't ship to a corporate buyer; the second is cold materialism dressed as philosophy. I build for the divine spark, and I say so in language a boardroom can hear: *Human Primacy* and *stewardship*.

## 1. The quiet erosion

We are engineering our own obsolescence — not with a bang, but with a thousand small surrenders of judgment. Nobody signs a contract handing their mind to the machine. They just stop doing the reps.

The cost of those skipped reps is now measurable, and the measurements are uncomfortable. In 2025, a team at the MIT Media Lab sat fifty-four people down to write essays — some with ChatGPT, some with a search engine, some with nothing but their own heads — and recorded their brain activity while they worked. The ChatGPT users showed the weakest neural connectivity of the three groups, and afterward they could barely recall or claim ownership of what they had supposedly written. Kosmyna and colleagues gave the phenomenon its name: cognitive debt [preprint; small sample — fifty-four people is a start, not a verdict]. A broader survey of 666 people found the same pattern at population scale: the more someone leaned on AI tools, the lower their critical-thinking scores, with cognitive offloading doing the mediating work (Gerlich, 2025) — though correlation is not causation, and I won't pretend it is. And when 319 knowledge workers were asked about their own habits, the ones most confident in generative AI reported thinking *less*, not more: the effort doesn't vanish, it migrates from doing the work to checking the machine's work (Lee et al., 2025) [self-reported, so read it as testimony, not telemetry].

If this feels unprecedented, the automation researchers would like a word. They have been documenting this exact failure mode since before most of today's AI founders were born. Parasuraman and Manzey's classic integration of the literature showed automation bias and complacency striking novices and experts alike — and, crucially, that it could not be trained away (2010) [classic]. You don't fix it with a workshop. Lyell and Coiera later found the bias actually *grows* with verification complexity: the harder the machine's output is to check, the more blindly we trust it (2017). Read that twice. It means the systems most in need of human oversight are the ones least likely to get it.

The human being in that loop has a name in the literature, and it isn't flattering. Elish (2019) called them "moral crumple zones": the people embedded in automated systems who absorb the moral and legal blame for failures they had no meaningful power to prevent. Picture it honestly — the operator who clicked "approve" on output they were never given time to read, then took the fall when it went wrong. The system worked as designed; the human was the design's shock absorber. Vallor (2015) described the slower version of the same decay: technologies that remove our chances to practice judgment don't just make us dependent, they atrophy the capacity itself. Moral deskilling. Use it or lose it, applied to conscience.

Notice how the two erosions feed each other. The less you practice judgment, the more you lean on the machine; the more you lean on the machine, the less attention you have left to check it — and Lyell and Coiera already told us the checking gets worse exactly when it matters most. It's a ratchet, not a cycle. Each turn tightens. The person at the end of that ratchet isn't augmented. They're administered — managed by systems they can neither understand nor refuse, holding the blame for outcomes they didn't choose. That's what I mean by *cognitive habitat degradation*, my term for it: not a single bad tool, but an environment in which the capacity for independent thought has nowhere left to stand.

And the substrate under all of it — attention — is being strip-mined. Ward's team showed that the mere presence of your own phone, powered off and face-down on the desk, measurably reduces your working memory (2017). Not using it. Just *there*, leaking capacity. Stothart and colleagues found that simply receiving a notification degrades your performance on attention tasks as much as stopping to answer the call (2015). Tim Wu, writing for lawyers rather than engineers, named the market plainly: human attention is a finite resource, and "attention brokers" harvest it in a marketplace almost none of us consented to join (2019). The rare randomized trial on the subject — Allcott's team paying people to deactivate Facebook for four weeks — found well-being went up and polarization went down (2020). The machine is not neutral about your mind. It has a business model for it, and the model is working.

Here is the frame I refuse to surrender, because it points at the way out. Back in 1998, Clark and Chalmers argued that cognition doesn't stop at the skull — it routinely *extends* into notebooks, instruments, software; the tools are part of the thinking, not separate from it [classic]. That is what Hybrid Intelligence should be: AI as scaffolding that extends human judgment, the way a good notebook extends memory. Not a substitute that quietly replaces the judgment while you're looking at your phone. Everything that follows is about building toward the first and refusing the second.

## 2. The power-wisdom gap

Our technological capabilities are vastly outpacing our human wisdom and governance frameworks. I call this the *power-wisdom gap* — my term, my framework — and I want to be clear it isn't a metaphor. It's a measurable institutional lag, and you can watch it play out in statute.

Take the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act: the most ambitious attempt on earth to govern this technology, and therefore the best available test of whether governance can keep pace. The early reviews are not encouraging. Veale and Zuiderveen Borgesius warned in 2021, before the ink was dry, that the Act's enforcement design could hollow out its own key provisions. Ebers showed in 2025 that several of the law's flagship provisions don't even follow the risk-based logic the statute claims as its organizing principle. And Carey, writing in 2026, argues the Act's big new category — systemic risk for general-purpose models — doesn't resolve uncertainty so much as institutionalize it, handing the decisive judgments to the very private actors the law was meant to discipline. Three scholars, five years, one direction: the law can't quite say what it means, or make anyone do it.

Liability is worse. Hacker (2023) took the EU's AI liability directives apart and found a half-hearted regime: narrow disclosure duties, no individual rights for the people actually harmed. Duffourc and Gerke (2023) followed the logic into the clinic and found the nightmare case made real — black-box medical AI can injure a patient with no viable path to liability under either strict or fault-based law. Read that plainly: the flagship legal regime of the AI age cannot reliably tell you who is responsible when the system fails. That is the gap, written into statute.

The deeper problem is structural, and the accountability literature has been circling it for a decade. Kroll and co-authors argued in 2017 that accountability has to be a *property of the system itself* — procedural regularity, transparency, due process built in — not a review stapled on after the harm [classic]. Ananny and Crawford answered the transparency enthusiasts in 2018: disclosure without an enforcement structure is performance, not accountability [classic]. You can publish the model card and change nothing. Raji's team built the serious version — an end-to-end framework for internal algorithmic auditing — and warned in the same paper that without structural teeth it devolves into theater (2020). Then Costanza-Chock, Raji, and Buolamwini went looking for the teeth: a field scan of 189 audit organizations found no shared standards at all, which means "independently audited" is currently an unverifiable claim (2022). Truby's group proposed the constructive way forward — regulatory sandboxes where rules and systems learn from each other instead of colliding after the fact (2022). And Bradford gave the reason none of this stays in Europe: the Brussels Effect means EU rules become the de facto global standard through sheer market force (2020) [classic]. The gaps above aren't Europe's problem. They're everyone's, exported at scale.

Why does a builder's essay spend this long on statutes? Because the gap is the business case. Every institution trying to deploy AI responsibly right now is doing it without a reliable legal map of who answers for what — which means the organizations that figure out governance first, structurally rather than theatrically, hold an advantage no model capability can copy. Compliance is usually treated as the tax on innovation. Here it's the moat: the people who can prove their systems are governed will be the ones allowed to operate them when the rules finally grow teeth. I'm building Glenride's infrastructure for that world — the one where "show me your audit trail" is the first question, not the last.

We are applying infinite computational leverage to fundamentally broken, bureaucratic institutions. The result is not progress. It is gridlock at the speed of light.

## 3. The extraction economy

I refuse to participate in this extraction. To refuse it intelligently, you have to name it precisely — and the naming has already been done, by people who paid close attention.

Shoshana Zuboff gave it the definitive name in 2019: surveillance capitalism, the economic order in which platform profits depend on extracting behavioral surplus — the data exhaust of your life — to predict and modify what you'll do next [classic book]. Four years earlier she had already put the mechanism in a single devastating phrase: these architectures "exile persons from their own behavior" (2015). You act; the platform owns the meaning of the act. Rahman and Thelen added the historical correction that matters most for builders: platform dominance was politically constructed, not technologically inevitable (2019). The rules were written by specific people, in specific rooms. They can be rewritten. Khan showed how the new power evades the old restraints — scale without profit, vertical integration, and data control combining into a form of dominance that existing antitrust doctrine literally cannot see, let alone restrain (2017). And Jones and Tonetti formalized the underlying economics: data is nonrival — my use doesn't diminish yours — which is exactly why firms hoard it; they fear the creative destruction its free circulation would unleash (2020). The hoarding isn't an accident of the business model. It *is* the business model.

The extraction runs on lock-in, and the lock-in is old enough to vote. Back in 2010, when most people still thought "the cloud" was a weather term, Armbrust and his co-authors named "data lock-in" through proprietary APIs as one of the top ten obstacles to cloud computing [classic]. Fifteen years later, Ofcom's market study put numbers on how that warning aged: AWS and Microsoft now hold 70–80% of UK cloud infrastructure, with egress fees — you pay to take your own data *out* — and interoperability barriers obstructing anyone who tries to leave [institutional report]. Cutolo and Kenney showed the power imbalance isn't a bug in the platform economy but intrinsic to its architecture: the platform's dominance over its ecosystem members is structural, not behavioral (2021). And Maffie and Hurtado gave the endgame its first peer-reviewed empirical treatment: platform decay, the cycle in which the platform serves users first, then sellers, then only itself (2025). Anyone who has watched a beloved tool get worse every quarter while the price goes up has lived inside that paper.

I've paid this tax myself, which is why I don't preach about it abstractly. Years ago I was organizing a conference on a deadline and needed working authentication fast, so I put the site on a hosted builder — pragmatic, quick, and completely someone else's. I convened the business leaders with cold emails anyway, and unwound the whole thing off the builder after. It did the job. But it was never mine: a tenant arrangement, not ownership. Tactical renting is fine. Mistaking the rental for the institution is the error. Every rented layer is a future negotiation where the landlord writes the terms. People call this paranoia. I call it having read the contract.

This is why independence is not an aesthetic for me. It is the load-bearing wall of everything I build. You cannot construct institutions that serve human flourishing on infrastructure designed to extract from humans — the incentives will eat the mission from the inside, slowly, then all at once. Every dependency is a lien on your mission. I keep my liens at zero.

## 4. Human Primacy

The solution is not to reject artificial intelligence. It is to bind it — permanently, structurally — beneath human moral agency. I call this *Human Primacy*: the conviction that human beings possess an intrinsic capacity for moral judgment, empathy, and creative wisdom that algorithms cannot replicate, and that our systems must be built to honor that hierarchy rather than quietly erode it. This is my framework term, and I mean it as doctrine, not decoration.

Philosophy got here first, and it got here rigorously. Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven formalized "meaningful human control" through two conditions that deserve to be better known: *tracking* — the system must act in line with the human's reasons — and *tracing* — the human must be able to reconstruct what the system did and why (2018). Control that can't do both isn't control; it's supervision theater. Amoroso and Tamburrini differentiated the principle into roles: the human as failsafe actor, as the attractor where accountability lands, and as the enactor of moral agency (2021). Veluwenkamp sharpened the demand further — control must track *normative* reasons, the right reasons; a system faithfully tracking the wrong reasons is morally compromised even with a human nominally in the loop (2022). And then Christen and colleagues issued the warning this entire essay takes most seriously: under time pressure and operational complexity, the control relation can *invert*, with the AI effectively steering the human operator (2023). The overseer becomes the ornament. Anyone building agent systems who hasn't confronted that paper is building blind.

Make it concrete. A system with tracking and tracing: you state your constraints, the agent acts inside them, and afterward you can reconstruct *why* it did what it did — the reasoning is inspectable, the blame assignable, the lesson learnable. A system without them: output arrives, fluent and confident, and when it's wrong nobody can say where the wrongness entered. The first system makes you smarter over time. The second makes you dependent on day one and helpless on day thirty. Most of what's sold as "AI oversight" today is the second system with a dashboard.

The institutions are converging on the same demand from the other direction. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework made lifecycle-wide governance — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — the American baseline in 2023 [institutional report]. Schmitz and colleagues argue that agentic AI specifically requires continuous, integrated supervision rather than episodic approvals — checkpoints at deployment are not governance of a system that keeps acting after deployment [workshop paper]. Mitchell, Ghosh, and Passi go further still: a nominal "human in the loop" is not effective oversight at all, because agent designs can quietly degrade the overseer's own judgment — and they propose structural correctives with teeth, including strategic friction, action gating, and bounded autonomy [position paper; advocacy framing, not consensus — but the direction is right].

The measurement science to enforce all of this already exists, which removes the last excuse. Schemmer's team built a two-dimensional measure of the "appropriateness of reliance" on AI advice — the metrology for governing hybrid human-machine teams [best paper, IUI '23]. Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos tested cognitive-forcing interventions and found they do reduce overreliance — with the sting in the tail that the most effective designs were the ones users liked least (2021). Sit with that: the safety feature that works best is the one the market punishes. Every product manager in AI should be forced to memorize that finding. Vasconcelos and colleagues found the constructive version: explanations reduce overreliance specifically when they make the AI's mistakes *cheap to verify* (2023). Governance works when checking is cheap. That sentence belongs on the wall of every AI lab on earth.

Regulated industry already lives under this logic, and I know it from the inside. The FDA's final guidance on predetermined change control plans for AI-enabled devices requires pre-specified, auditable change control for adaptive models — you declare in advance what the model is allowed to become, and you prove it stayed inside the lines (2024/2025) [guidance]. Its draft lifecycle guidance goes further, expecting data lineage, bias analysis, and evaluation of human–AI team performance as a unit (2025) [draft — directional expectations, not settled requirements]. I spent over a decade in regulated clinical data operations. I know what it means to govern a system where failure harms people: audit trails, verification, no shortcuts, no "move fast" exemptions. Agent infrastructure gets the same standard. No exceptions for novelty.

## 5. The Agentic Enterprise

I am an Independent Architect. I build independent ecosystems that distill chaos into actionable output. The organizational form I'm building toward I call the *Agentic Enterprise* — my framework term for the shift of human labor from manual production to intent engineering and system governance: specifying what should be built, constraining how autonomous systems build it, and verifying the result.

The technical substrate has a name too: the *Multi-Agent Collaborative Thought Graph*, or MaCTG. Specialized agents — planner, builder, reviewer — coordinated under human orchestration, with verification *between* agents as the error-control mechanism. This is my architecture, but it stands on active research ground, and I want to show the receipts. Microsoft Research's AutoGen work introduced the "conversable agent" abstraction — flexible conversation topologies, two-agent chats, group chats with managers, nested workflows — with human-in-the-loop participation as a first-class pattern rather than a bolt-on (Wu et al., 2023) [preprint]. Du and colleagues then demonstrated why the multi-agent structure matters for truth: when LLM instances propose, critique, and converge over rounds of debate, reasoning and factual validity improve significantly over single-agent baselines (2024) [peer-reviewed, ICML]. The survey literature (Guo et al., 2024) maps the broader field — how agents are profiled, how they communicate, how they're evaluated [preprint survey]. And the design rationale for distributing the load across agents instead of piling it onto one human operator reaches back to Sweller's classic constraint: human working memory is severely limited under parallel complexity, so any serious orchestration architecture must minimize what it demands of the human supervisor (1988) [classic]. The machine workforce scales; the human governor doesn't. Design accordingly.

A practical barrier I think about constantly is what I call *Cold Start Context Loss* — the slow leak of high-value constraints and project history into fragmented model conversations, where they become unfindable exactly when they matter. The empirical basis for worrying about this is now solid, and it arrives as a chain. First the phenomenon: Liu and colleagues demonstrated the U-shaped "lost in the middle" curve — information buried mid-context is severely degraded, even in models explicitly built for long contexts (2024) [peer-reviewed]. Then the mechanism: Hsieh's team identified it as intrinsic positional attention bias, an architectural property rather than a prompting failure (2024) [peer-reviewed]. Then the pervasiveness: Guo and Vosoughi found primacy and recency effects across tasks and models, resistant to prompt-based mitigation (2024) [preprint]. Phenomenon, mechanism, pervasiveness. The conclusion writes itself: long context windows do not equal reliable use of long context, so production agent systems must treat context as a managed resource — distilled, structured, re-injected. That is precisely the function my ContextForge pipeline serves. Engineering judgment, informed by findings — not deduced from them, and I won't claim otherwise.

What MaCTG looks like in practice, since I get asked: a planner agent decomposes the intent into verifiable steps; builder agents execute against those steps with the constraints injected fresh each time — because we know the middle of the context is where instructions go to die; reviewer agents check the output against the original intent before anything ships. The human — me, the orchestrator — sets the intent, defines the constraints, and holds the only merge key. Nothing reaches the world without passing a reviewer that isn't the builder and a human who isn't the machine. It's slower than letting one agent run wild. It's also the difference between an enterprise and an accident.

Now the honest accounting on what AI actually does to work, because the literature refuses the simple story in both directions and so do I. On the optimistic side, the numbers are real: Noy and Zhang's randomized trial found ChatGPT cut professional writing time by 40% and raised quality by 18% — while noting the tool substituted for effort rather than complementing skill (2023) [peer-reviewed, *Science*]. Brynjolfsson's team tracked 5,179 support agents resolving 14% more issues per hour — 34% for novices — with better retention and sentiment to boot (2025) [peer-reviewed, QJE]. Peng and colleagues ran the Copilot trial: developers finished tasks 55.8% faster, with the least experienced gaining most (2023) [preprint RCT]. These are not vibes. They're measurements.

But the same research program keeps producing the counterweight, and intellectual honesty requires equal airtime for it. Dell'Acqua's group put 758 BCG consultants on GPT-4 and found them 25% faster *inside* the AI's frontier — and 19% *less accurate* outside it (2023) [preprint]. The tool has a jagged edge, and people bleed on it. The same lab found recruiters given *higher-quality* AI were *less* accurate — blind acceptance, less effort, worse outcomes (Dell'Acqua, 2022) [preprint]. Then the team result nobody in the industry wanted: inserting an automated teammate into human teams *decreased* performance, through coordination failures and eroding trust (Dell'Acqua, Kogut, & Perkowski, 2025) [peer-reviewed]. And Becker's team at METR ran the trial that should humble every AI maximalist: experienced open-source developers were 19% *slower* with AI assistance while believing they were 20% *faster* (2025) [preprint RCT]. Confidence in the tool is not evidence of the tool. Write that on the other wall.

The benchmarks exist to keep everyone honest, and they tell a consistent story if you read them straight. SWE-bench grades agents on 2,294 real GitHub issues with execution-based verification (Jimenez et al., 2024) [peer-reviewed]. GAIA's 466 real-world tool-use questions: humans score 92%, GPT-4 with plugins manages 15% (Mialon et al., 2023) [preprint]. WebArena's multi-step web tasks: the best agent hits 14.41% against a human 78.24% (Zhou et al., 2023) [preprint]. OSWorld's 369 tasks inside real operating systems: humans 72.36%, best model 12.24% (Xie et al., 2024) [preprint]. MLAgentBench's ML experiments: the best agent completes 37.5% (Huang et al., 2023) [preprint]. τ-bench tests conversational agents against simulated users enforcing real retail and airline policies (Yao et al., 2024) [preprint]. The honest read across all six: agents are powerful inside bounded frontiers and unreliable outside them — which is exactly why the Agentic Enterprise puts verification, not capability, at the center of the design. Bansal's team supplied the reason in one clean result: explanations increase people's acceptance of AI output *regardless of whether the output is correct* (2021) [peer-reviewed, CHI]. Fluency is not fidelity. Governance has to be built for the gap between them.

The macro picture counsels ambition with humility, in that order. Eloundou and colleagues estimated that roughly 80% of the U.S. workforce could see at least a tenth of their tasks affected by LLMs, with higher-wage cognitive jobs the most exposed (2023) [preprint]. Acemoglu bounded the ten-year productivity gain at roughly 0.66% of total factor productivity — judgment-heavy tasks resist automation, and economies are mostly judgment (2025) [peer-reviewed]. And Dell'Acqua's NBER working paper found individuals paired with AI matching the output of two-person teams, dissolving the old R&D–commercial silos in the process (2025) [preprint]. The opportunity is real. So is the hype cycle. I build for the first and refuse the second — and the benchmarks are how I tell them apart.

## 6. Digital autonomy: own the stack

Governance principles are difficult to honor inside infrastructure you do not control. This is the architectural conviction beneath everything I build: absolute digital autonomy. You cannot build independent institutions on rented, extractive infrastructure — the landlord's incentives will eventually override the tenant's mission, because that is what landlords are for.

The technical articulation of the alternative comes from the local-first software movement, and Kleppmann's team stated it canonically: the primary copy of your data lives on your device, the cloud is a sync peer rather than a custodian, and you own your data in spite of the cloud (2019) [classic]. My implementation choices follow directly — embedded local databases journaling every agent action, local model inference over my own hardware, self-hosted deployment with no third-party wrappers in the critical path. Is local-first superior in general? Contested, and I'll say so. That it preserves the operator's control over data and execution is definitional. I optimize for control.

The economics of openness support the bet, and they cut both ways in instructive fashion. Start with the value: Hoffmann, Nagle, and Zhou estimated open source's supply-side value at $4.15 billion against a demand-side replacement figure of $8.8 trillion — what firms would have to spend to rebuild it all themselves (2024) [preprint; and note carefully that the $8.8T is a replacement *estimate*, not measured value]. The European Commission's study found roughly a billion euros invested in open source in 2018 producing €65–95 billion in EU GDP impact (Blind et al., 2021) [institutional report]. Nagle showed the counterintuitive kicker: firms that *contribute* to open source capture up to 100% more productive value from it than free-riders — generosity pays (2018) [peer-reviewed]. Then the fragility, because a commons this valuable deserves an honest audit: Eghbal documented open source as digital public infrastructure maintained largely by underpaid volunteers — a textbook public-goods incentive failure (2016) [institutional report]. Avelino's team quantified how thin the ice is: of 1,932 popular GitHub projects, 16% had already suffered "truck factor developer detachment," and 55% have a truck factor of exactly one — one person walking away from the project ending it (2019) [peer-reviewed]. Open infrastructure is a commons. Commons need stewards, or they become ruins with good documentation.

Lock-in, I've learned, is not just a technical problem. It's a legal one wearing a technical costume. Wong and Henderson tested data portability in practice — 230 real requests under the GDPR's Article 20 — and only 74.8% completed (2019) [peer-reviewed]. Portability on paper; lock-in in practice. Graef, Husovec, and Purtova analyzed portability as a competition instrument against data-holder power (2018) [peer-reviewed], and De Hert's team framed it as the stimulus for interoperable, user-centric services (2018) [peer-reviewed] — the law wants an exit door, but the architecture keeps bricking it up. Rozenshtein showed the decentralized alternative working as designed: federation gives users genuine "freedom of exit," with networks self-policing through bottom-up instance governance (2023) [peer-reviewed]. But De Filippi and Loveluck supplied the caveat my own position requires me to carry: decentralization does not automatically dissolve power concentration — there is governance *by* infrastructure and governance *of* it, and confusing the two is a category error (2016) [peer-reviewed]. Owning your servers doesn't make you free; it makes you responsible. I prefer responsibility. Gomulkiewicz asked whether right-to-repair must extend to software — because owned infrastructure means nothing without the legal right to inspect and fix the code running on it (2022) [peer-reviewed].

A terminology note, since words are load-bearing for me: there is a substantial European literature on the democratic stakes of who controls digital infrastructure — Floridi (2020), Pohle and Thiel (2020), Roberts and colleagues (2021) all work this terrain [peer-reviewed]. I draw on their analysis while using my own term, because in American political discourse their banner word signals something I do not mean and will not ship to a corporate buyer. My term is *digital autonomy*: full ownership of the stack, from routing to compute, with no landlord standing between the institution and its mission.

This is why the Glenride Institute runs on ThinkTank OS — a proprietary, local-first platform built explicitly for our scholars to plan, write, edit, and publish the research manuscripts at the core of our mission. Let me be precise about what that is and isn't: ThinkTank OS is the manuscript engine, the institution's working memory. It is not the software factory. The factory builds the tools; ThinkTank OS is where the thinking gets written down, argued over, and shipped.

## 7. The independent institute

Why a think tank, and why independent? Because the institutions we have are very good at administering research and increasingly bad at producing breakthroughs — and the literature says so from the inside, which is the only testimony that counts.

Start with the productivity numbers, because they're stark. Bloom and colleagues found research effort rising steeply while research productivity declines: sustaining Moore's Law today requires roughly eighteen times more researchers than it did in the early 1970s (2020) [peer-reviewed, AER]. Park, Leahey, and Funk examined 45 million papers and 3.9 million patents and found work becoming less disruptive over time — more consolidation, fewer ruptures (2023) [peer-reviewed, *Nature*; known methodological critiques here, so read it as the headline finding, not gospel]. Chu and Evans showed the mechanism from the inside: in large fields, paper deluges ossify the canon rather than overturning it — volume becomes its own kind of conservatism (2021) [peer-reviewed, PNAS]. And Wu, Wang, and Evans found the pattern's photographic negative: small, independent teams — not big consortia — are the ones producing disruptive work (2019) [peer-reviewed, *Nature*]. Bigness administers. Smallness disrupts.

The mechanism is bureaucratic, and the witnesses are impeccable. Alberts, Kirschner, Tilghman, and Varmus — four of the most decorated biomedical scientists alive — wrote in 2014 that hypercompetition and perverse funding incentives are actively discouraging risk in American biomedical research [peer-reviewed, PNAS]. When the establishment's own laureates say the system punishes boldness, believe them. Rockwell surveyed 6,081 federally funded principal investigators and found 42% of grant-supported research time going to administrative tasks (2009) [peer-reviewed]. Sit with that number the way a working scientist has to: nearly half the week — the week the grants are supposedly *funding* — spent feeding the machine that feeds the machine. Pier's team replicated NIH peer review and found essentially *zero* agreement among reviewers evaluating the same proposals (2018) [peer-reviewed, PNAS]. The gatekeeping lottery is not even consistent about its own standards. Bol, de Vaan, and van de Rijt documented the Matthew effect in funding: researchers just above a funding threshold accumulated twice the later funding of near-identical near-misses — Merton's 1968 cumulative-advantage finding, still compounding more than half a century later (2018; Merton, 1968) [peer-reviewed, PNAS; classic]. Fortin and Currie showed impact-per-dollar *falls* as grant size grows: spreading many smaller grants beats concentrating on "excellence" (2013) [peer-reviewed]. Nguyen's team found the "super-PIs" — investigators holding three or more NIH grants — tripled between 1991 and 2020, capturing 28% of all funding, with the inequities compounding along gender and racial lines (2023) [peer-reviewed, JAMA]. The OECD described the postdoc precariat — short contracts, low pay, no security — as the *majority condition* of academic research careers (2021) [institutional report]. And the U.S. GAO put the numbers on it: median postdoc pay around $60–62K, versus roughly $118.5K for comparable doctorate holders outside academia (2026) [institutional report]. We are training brilliant people for a decade and then paying them like interns. The wonder isn't that talent leaves. It's that any stays.

The alternatives are known, and some have been tested. Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Manso compared HHMI investigators — long-term money, explicit tolerance for failure — against matched NIH grantees, and found the HHMI scientists producing high-impact papers at far higher rates (2011) [peer-reviewed]. Patience outperforms surveillance. Azoulay's team later dissected the ARPA model's working parts: administrative flexibility, empowered program directors, no committee consensus required (2019) [peer-reviewed]. And when peer review itself is the bottleneck, Avin (2019) and Fang and Casadevall (2016) made the epistemic case for the heretical option: triage proposals into meritorious and unmeritorious, then run a modified lottery among the fundable pool [peer-reviewed]. Randomness among the qualified beats false precision among the connected.

History supplies the existence proof for the protected lab. Gertner chronicled Bell Labs' sheltered environment incubating the transistor, information theory, and the laser — the substrate of the modern world, built by people who were told to think (2012) [book]. Hiltzik showed PARC's sheltered lab producing the personal computer, the graphical interface, Ethernet, and laser printing — and then showed corporate headquarters starving it, the inventions walking out the door to make other companies' fortunes (1999) [book]. Mowery documented the aftermath in the economics literature: the post-1985 restructuring and retreat of the American corporate research lab, the deliberate dismantling of the model that had produced the modern world (2009) [peer-reviewed]. Nothing replaced it. That vacancy is the opportunity.

The replacement is being prototyped right now. Marblestone and colleagues proposed Focused Research Organizations for the exact gap where academia, venture capital, and government labs all fail: mid-scale, team-based, public-good science infrastructure — too applied for universities, too slow for VCs, too weird for agencies (2022) [peer-reviewed, *Nature*]. Three years later, the field notes arrived: nearly a dozen FROs launched, $20–50 million each, 10–30 staff, three-to-seven-year terms, with direct design lessons for anyone attempting the model (Marblestone et al., 2025) [institutional report, National Academies' policy journal]. The design lessons I take from all of this are specific. Keep the grants small and the terms short — Fortin and Currie's finding that impact-per-dollar falls as grants grow is a design constraint, not trivia. Tolerate failure explicitly, the HHMI way, because breakthrough work looks like wasted money until it doesn't. Strip the administration to zero: if Rockwell's 42% is the disease, the cure is infrastructure that absorbs the paperwork before a scholar ever sees it — which is exactly what ThinkTank OS is for. And publish to decision-makers, not just journals, because a brilliant brief nobody in power reads is a tree falling in an empty forest. Glenride's fellowship — small grants, protected time, machine-absorbed administration, briefs that land on desks that matter — is these findings assembled into an institution.

Glenride is my entry in this lineage: an independent institute that convenes scholars, protects their time by letting machines absorb the administration, and ships their work to decision-makers instead of journals. Give brilliant people the space to build whatever the work demands, and get the bureaucracy out of their way. That is the whole theory, and it has a pedigree.

## 8. The stakes: supply chains and talent

A framework is only as serious as the problems it takes on. Two are structural, measurable, and getting worse while we debate terminology.

First, critical mineral supply chains are concentrating, not diversifying — the trend line points the wrong way and it's accelerating. The IEA's 2025 outlook found refining concentration *rising*: the top-three-country share moved from 82% to 86% in just four years, with China the leading refiner for 19 of 20 strategic minerals [institutional report]. The USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries put the American position in plain numbers: import reliance above 50% of apparent consumption for 46 nonfuel mineral commodities, and 100% reliance — total dependence — for 15 of them (2025) [institutional report]. The Department of Energy's 2023 assessment rated 38 energy-critical materials by supply-risk criticality through 2035, formalizing what the market already knew [institutional report]. And the general supply-chain literature confirms this isn't a minerals-only story. Gebhardt's team, surveying experts after COVID, found structural vulnerability demanding the unglamorous disciplines: mapping, collaboration, risk-weighted supplier selection (2022) [peer-reviewed]. Sako described the deeper design flaw: hyper-optimized, globally stretched supply chains are fragile *by construction* — efficiency ate the redundancy (2022) [peer-reviewed]. Ağca and colleagues quantified the financial transmission: firms with Chinese supply-chain partners suffered 12–13% abnormal spikes in credit risk (2023) [peer-reviewed]. Shi's team judged just-in-time models to carry "fatal risks" under long-term disruption — not inefficiencies, *fatal* risks (2023) [peer-reviewed].

The much-discussed remedies, honestly examined, have second-order effects of their own. Charpin and Cousineau showed that friendshoring — moving supply chains to allied countries — actually *increases* supply-base complexity and hidden tier-2 supplier sharing, creating new fragilities that demand new visibility (2024) [peer-reviewed]. You diversify the map and concentrate the plumbing. Luo and Wang documented the American corporate response since 2018: the steady shift toward "China plus N" sourcing, near-shoring, friend-shoring (2026) [peer-reviewed]. Grover and Vézina found geopolitical distance increasingly determining where investment flows at all, the effect strengthening after the pandemic (2026) [peer-reviewed]. And Ahn and Tan, writing for the IMF, did the cost-benefit analysis the debate needed: diversifying China-bloc-exposed imports costs roughly 0.02% of welfare while cutting fragmentation-scenario losses by 12% (2025) [institutional report]. Resilience is cheap relative to the catastrophe it insures against — the most lopsided trade in public policy, and we still underbuy it. Gopal's team made the semiconductor case specifically: chain fragility answered by GIS- and AI-enabled visibility — institutional intelligence as the direct response (2022) [peer-reviewed]. See the pattern: every serious answer to supply-chain fragility is an *information* answer. Somebody has to build the institution that produces it.

Second, the talent base is leaking — and here too the numbers refuse the comforting story. The NSF's Science and Engineering Indicators benchmark the American STEM workforce at 36.8 million people and track the dimensions that determine whether it endures: foreign-born participation, stay rates of U.S.-trained scientists and engineers (National Science Board, 2024) [institutional report]. Xie and colleagues documented what happens when a country treats its own researchers as suspects: departures of U.S.-based Chinese-descent scientists rose 75% after the 2018 China Initiative, with survey data showing a majority of the remainder considering leaving (2023) [peer-reviewed, PNAS]. The AI-specific drain runs sharper. Gofman and Jin traced AI professors leaving for industry between 2004 and 2018 and found the affected universities producing fewer AI startups and attracting less funding — the departure lounge has a measurable cost to the institution left behind (2024) [peer-reviewed, *Journal of Finance*]. Jurowetzki's team found the private sector outright hoarding AI researchers, with the young and highly cited most likely to leave — and their post-transition work declining in novelty and disruptiveness (2025) [peer-reviewed]. Akcigit and colleagues put the market-clearing price on the exodus: by 2019, 68% of AI researchers were in industry, top-1% industry pay exceeding academia by $1.5 million, with movers publishing 65% less and patenting 530% more (2026) [preprint]. And Zaslavsky and Akbaritabar added the grimmest dimension of all: studying Ukraine from 2009 to 2022, they showed conflict reorganizing an entire national science system — a reminder that researcher mobility is not always voluntary "brain circulation"; sometimes it's evacuation (2026) [peer-reviewed].

Glenride's fellowship model — micro-grants commissioning policy briefs from researchers, a room of their own, the administration absorbed by machines — is my proposed answer to both halves of this section: the supply-chain questions need independent institutional intelligence, and the talent questions need institutions worth staying for. Its efficacy is untested. I present it as a pilot, not a result, because the whole point of this essay is that claims come with receipts or they don't come at all.

## 9. Flourishing stewardship

We are not just building software. We are proving a model — and a model is only proved by living inside it.

The argument of this essay, compressed to its load-bearing walls: ungoverned AI displaces human judgment in measurable ways, from EEG traces to critical-thinking scores; our governance institutions lag our capabilities by design rather than accident, and the flagship statute of the AI age can't reliably assign responsibility; the platform economy extracts from the attention it harvests, then locks the doors behind it; the technical answer is human-governed agent systems running on infrastructure their operators actually own; the institutional answer is independent institutes that protect scholars' time and ship their work to power instead of journals; and the stakes are the supply chains civilization runs on and the minds that understand them.

*Flourishing stewardship* is my name for the end state — my framework term, my north star. Humans restored as the thoughtful architects of a sustainable planet. Technology scaling human flourishing rather than extracting from it. Scholars given the unbroken time to think, operators given intelligence they can trust, builders given infrastructure nobody can take away.

The literature cited here does not prove that end state attainable. I want to be explicit about what 123 sources do and don't establish: they prove the component problems are real, measurable, and under-governed. They prove the failure modes have names, mechanisms, and numbers attached. They do not prove my answer works — no paper can, because the answer hasn't been built yet at the scale I'm describing. What they prove is that the people closest to these problems have been documenting them, on the record, for years, while the institutions responsible mostly scheduled another workshop.

None of this asks you to take my word for anything. That's the point of the 123 citations — not to impress, but to make every load-bearing claim checkable. Disagree with the framework? The sources are all listed; argue with them instead of me. What I won't do is ask you to trust the institution I'm building while exempting myself from its standard. The essay you're reading *is* the standard, applied to its author first.

I got one good idea and asked God for the discipline to carry it. This essay is me modeling the standard before I ask it of anyone: argue plainly, cite honestly, build independently, and let the work be checked. Glenride will ask nothing of its scholars that its founder has not first done himself. The room is being built. The door is open.
